Accessible travel in national parks works best when you plan from the official information rather than from photographs. What is reachable, what is smooth, and what requires assistance varies enormously between parks and even between areas of the same park. Start by asking the park directly, then build the days around what is confirmed.

## Ask for the specific detail you need

General statements about accessibility rarely answer the real question. Ask about surface type and grade on the paths you plan to use, whether restrooms along the way are accessible, where accessible parking sits relative to the entrance, and whether the shuttle or transit you intend to use has accessible vehicles.

Request written or recorded answers so you can refer back to them, and ask what has changed recently. Maintenance, construction, and seasonal closures all affect access.

## Plan around energy, not just distance

A route may be short on a map and still involve heat, elevation, or uneven ground that drains you faster than expected. Build in rest stops, shade, and a place to sit, and plan a shorter version of each day that you would be happy with if energy runs low.

Weather changes the calculus too. Heat and cold affect different people differently, and a plan that works at one temperature may not work at another. Have an indoor or sheltered alternative ready for every outdoor stop.

## Use the services that already exist

Many parks offer accessible shuttles, lending equipment, accessible viewpoints, and visitor centres with seating and staff who know the terrain. These services are not always advertised prominently, so ask when you arrive and check whether you need to book ahead.

Lodging inside the park, where available, can remove a long daily commute. If it is full, look for accessible options on the approach routes and confirm the details with the property rather than relying on a listing.

## Check the details that photographs will not show

A viewpoint may be paved and still involve a steep approach, a narrow passing space, or a bench that faces the wrong way. Ask how many rest stops sit between the parking area and the view, and whether anyone in your group will need to wait somewhere shaded while the rest of the group walks on.

Surface condition changes with weather too. Gravel, packed earth, and boardwalk all behave differently when wet, and the same path can be easy in the morning and difficult after rain. Ask what the surface is like after a storm, and whether anyone clears it.

## Build in a buffer for the unexpected

Allow extra time for transfers, narrower windows for services that run on a schedule, and a plan for what happens if your first choice is closed. Carrying a backup idea for each day keeps the trip from collapsing when one element changes.

### Verify before you go
- Read the accessibility pages for your park on [nps.gov](https://www.nps.gov).
- Check accessible camping and lodging options on [recreation.gov](https://www.recreation.gov).
- Contact the park directly with the specific surface, restroom, and transport questions you have.
- Confirm shuttle availability, equipment loans, and any booking they require.
- Write down your shorter daily plan and your indoor alternative for each stop.
- Note the name and opening status of the accessible restrooms along your route.
