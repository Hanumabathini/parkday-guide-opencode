A first national park trip goes better when you plan fewer things and confirm them properly. Most first-timers overfill the schedule and undercheck the logistics, which turns a simple visit into a day of driving and queues. Work in this order: pick the park, pick the days, then arrange the details.

## Choose one park and one base

Trying to string together several parks in one trip usually means more time on roads than on trails. Pick a single park and a single base location near it, then decide how many days you need. Two or three full days gives you a rest day, a weather buffer, and enough time to reach the areas that sit furthest from the entrance.

If you have limited mobility or are travelling with young children or older relatives, make that the deciding factor when you choose the park rather than an afterthought once you arrive.

## Let access rules shape the itinerary

Check early whether your dates fall inside a period when the park requires reservations, timed entry, or a permit for the areas you want to see. These requirements are set per park and per season, and they change. If a requirement applies, arrange it before you book anything else, because the rest of the plan depends on it. Keep the confirmation somewhere you can reach without signal, since coverage inside parks is often poor.

The same applies to lodging and camping. In-park places to stay and camp are limited and often released on a schedule that differs from park to park. Read the park's own page for how booking works there, and keep a fallback base outside the entrance in case the in-park option is full. Book something flexible until the rest of the plan settles, so a change of dates does not cost you twice.

## Plan days by area, not by attraction list

Group what you want to see by geography so you are not crossing the park repeatedly. Give each day a rough area, a couple of anchor stops, and open time for weather, rest, and unplanned stops. First visits rarely survive an hour-by-hour schedule, and a plan with slack is easier to adjust when a road closes or a storm rolls in.

Leave the first morning deliberately light. Entrance stations, orientation films, and visitor centre conversations take longer than you expect and usually save you time later.

## Pack for the actual conditions, not the brochure

Read the park's own guidance on weather, wildlife, and current hazards before you pack. Water, layers, sun protection, and a small first-aid kit are the baseline almost everywhere. Add whatever the park's elevation, exposure, or season demands, and confirm whether any facilities you were counting on are open.

### Verify before you go
- Confirm entry requirements, alerts, and road status on [nps.gov](https://www.nps.gov).
- Check availability for camping and permits on [recreation.gov](https://www.recreation.gov).
- Read the specific park page for its seasonal hours and current guidance.
- Reconfirm your lodging, your reservation or permit, and your route the day before.
- Decide in advance what you will do if your planned area is closed, and where you will go instead.
